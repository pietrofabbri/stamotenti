title: "Therapeutic Culture"
---

Words like “process,” “boundaries,” or “self-care” originated in psychologists’ offices. Today, you hear them at work, at home, and among friends. People who use them often aren’t in therapy, but they talk about themselves in the way that therapy has made it commonplace to do so.

## Essential Definition

**“Therapeutic culture” refers to the spread—especially in the 20th century—of language and practices of psychological and psychotherapeutic origin far beyond the clinical setting, to the point of becoming an ordinary way of interpreting one’s life, emotions, and relationships.** At the heart of this culture lies inner well-being as a goal.

## History of the Term

The Greek *therapeúein*, from which “therapy” derives, primarily means “to be at the service of, to provide assistance.” As early as Homer, it referred to a man’s retinue (*Odyssey*, XIII, 265). From this sense of service, the *Liddell-Scott-Jones*—the standard dictionary of Ancient Greek—derives various and equally ancient uses: service rendered to the gods, that is, worship (Hesiod, *Works and Days*, 135; Herodotus, II, 37); deference toward the powerful, sometimes in the negative sense of flattery (Herodotus, III, 80); the handling of a practical matter; the care of the body and the fields; and, in Hippocrates and Thucydides (II, 47 and 51, where he describes the plague of Athens), the care of the sick. The dictionary does not arrange these uses in chronological order. The priest who serves and the doctor who heals share the same word, and the underlying idea is that of someone who makes themselves available to someone or something. In English, *therapeutic* is attested from around 1640 and *therapy* from 1846, from the Modern Latin *therapia* (Online Etymology Dictionary).

“Culture” comes from the Latin *colere*, “to cultivate, to inhabit.” According to de Vaan, *colere* goes back to the reconstructed Indo-European root *\*kʷel-*, “to move, to circle around,” from which “to inhabit a place” and then “to work it” (Treccani gives only the derivation from *colere*, with the participle *cultus*). The agricultural meaning had already taken on a figurative sense by the time of Cicero, who in the *Tusculanae Disputations* (II, 13) compares the soul to a fertile but uncultivated field, which yields no fruit without cultivation, and concludes: “the cultivation of the soul is philosophy” (*cultura autem animi philosophia est*; my translation). In the nineteenth century, the term took on the ethnographic meaning of a people’s collective customs and achievements (Online Etymology Dictionary).

The expression “therapeutic culture” refers to a twentieth-century phenomenon and is linked, in the field of sociology, to Philip Rieff, who in 1966 spoke of the “triumph of the therapeutic” and the “psychological man.” A more recent critical analysis is provided by Frank Furedi in *Therapy Culture* (2004).

## In Context

In 1966, Philip Rieff described the emergence of the “psychological man,” a new anthropological type who replaces the search for religious salvation or moral character with the pursuit of inner well-being through psychological means.

Rieff’s interpretation is one of several possible ones. Eva Illouz, in her study of therapeutic culture, places Rieff among the authors of “communitarian” criticism, alongside Trilling, Lasch, and Cushman. According to this critique, therapeutic thought has contributed to the decline of communal life by promoting an individualism presented as self-care.

Illouz employs a different method. She examines how therapeutic discourse actually operates in the workplace, in marriage, and in self-help groups, without deciding in advance whether it liberates or constrains those who adopt it. She also discusses Michel Foucault’s interpretation, according to which therapeutic confession constitutes a “political technology of the self.”

The link to individualism is explicit in the work of Robert Bellah and his co-authors. In their distinction between “hard” and “soft” individualism, the “soft,” expressive form is often experienced in therapeutic terms. Therapeutic culture is thus one of the contexts in which American individualism transformed during the twentieth century.
