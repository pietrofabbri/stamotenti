title: "Mindfulness"
---

Imagine watching the thoughts that pass through your mind like clouds crossing the sky: you notice them, acknowledge their presence, but don’t immediately rush to chase after them or judge them as “good” or “bad.” You let them pass while remaining, as much as possible, the sky that observes them.

## Essential Definition

**“Mindfulness” is the English word most commonly used today to refer to a specific form of attention: noticing what is happening, both within oneself and around oneself, moment by moment, without reacting immediately and without immediately judging what is observed.** It is one of many families of meditation (see the entry on *Meditation*), not a synonym for “meditation” in general.

## Context of the Entry

Since the 1980s, the English word “mindfulness” has been the most widespread translation of the Pali term *sati* (see the entry on *sati*). However, this translation narrows the original meaning: in traditional Buddhism, the term has a broader and more ethically oriented meaning than is often presented today. **Rupert Gethin’s philological research (2011) shows that in ancient texts, *sati* also functions as a form of “moral memory”: actively remembering to behave well, not merely observing what happens in a neutral way.**

There is also a second historical origin of the term, independent of Buddhism: psychologist Ellen Langer’s research (*Mindfulness*, 1989) on mindfulness as an alternative to automatic and habitual thinking. This line of research differs from the one pioneered by Jon Kabat-Zinn (see the entry on *MBSR*), and **generalist treatments tend to ignore it: they present the history of the term as if it had a single origin—the Buddhist one—when in reality it has at least two, which arose independently.**

This dual background explains a tension in today’s most widespread clinical definition—that of mindfulness as a “nonjudgmental” attitude toward any mental content. **Gethin (2011) points out the risk: suggesting that all mental states have the same value.** But in the original Buddhist framework, this is not the case: certain states, such as greed, are explicitly to be corrected, not merely observed neutrally.

A referee who merely observes the game without ever intervening represents mindfulness as “neutral observation.” A coach who observes but also keeps in mind the direction in which the team should improve is mindfulness as the “reminder” of a direction to follow. The most widespread clinical version today resembles the former; the traditional Buddhist version, according to this interpretation, resembles the latter.
