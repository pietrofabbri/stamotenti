title: "Mindfulness"
---

Picture watching thoughts pass through the mind like clouds crossing the sky: you notice them, you recognize that they are there, but you do not immediately jump to chase them or judge them as "good" or "bad." You let them pass while you remain, as much as possible, the sky that observes them.

## Essential Definition

"Mindfulness" is the English word most commonly used today to describe a specific form of attention: noticing what is happening, within oneself and around oneself, moment by moment, without reacting immediately and without immediately judging what is observed. It is one of many families of meditation (see the *Meditation* entry), not a synonym for "meditation" in general.

## In Context

Since the 1980s, the English word "mindfulness" has been the most common translation of the Pali term *sati* (see dedicated entry). But the translation narrows the original meaning: in traditional Buddhism the term has a broader sense, and one more oriented toward ethics, than how it is often presented today. Rupert Gethin's philological research (2011) shows that in ancient texts *sati* also functions as a form of "moral memory": actively remembering to behave well, not merely observing what happens in a neutral way.

There is also a second, independent historical origin of the term: psychologist Ellen Langer's research (*Mindfulness*, 1989) on mental presence as an alternative to automatic, habitual thinking. This is a separate line of research from the one originating with Jon Kabat-Zinn (see the *MBSR* entry), and general-audience treatments tend to ignore it: they tell the history of the term as if it had a single origin, the Buddhist one, when in fact it has at least two, which arose independently.

This double background explains a tension in today's most common clinical definition, that of mindfulness as a "non-judgmental" attitude toward any mental content. Gethin (2011) points out the risk: it can suggest that all mental states have equal value. But this is not so within the original Buddhist framework: some states, such as greed, remain explicitly something to correct, not merely to observe neutrally.

A referee who merely watches the game without ever intervening is mindfulness as "neutral observation." A coach who watches but also keeps in mind the direction in which the team should improve is mindfulness as "memory" of a direction to follow. Today's most common clinical version resembles the former more closely; the traditional Buddhist version, on this reading, resembles the latter more closely.
