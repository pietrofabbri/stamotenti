---
title: "Sīla (Buddhist Ethics)"
---

Think of *sīla* as the foundation of a house: it is not the most visible or interesting part of the building, but without a stable foundation the rest — the walls, the roof, everything built on top — does not hold, or holds up far less well.

## Essential Definition

*Sīla* is the Pali word for "ethical conduct": the set of principles that govern how one behaves toward others and toward the world, within the Buddhist tradition. It is not a rule imposed from outside, detached from the rest of the practice: it is one of the three classical components of the Buddhist path, together with concentration (*samādhi*) and understanding (*paññā*).

## In Context

The Pali canon does not present *sīla* as a simple piece of advice, but as the first link in a causal chain. The Cetana Sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2) describes eleven steps in sequence: ethical conduct produces freedom from remorse; from freedom from remorse arise joy and serenity; only then does mental concentration become possible, and from this, understanding. The Sonadanda Sutta (Dīgha-Nikāya 4) conveys the same idea with a more direct image: virtue and understanding "wash" each other, as one hand washes the other.

Two key academic studies confirm this link, although they describe it with slightly different models. Damien Keown (*The Nature of Buddhist Ethics*, 1992) argues that the truly inseparable pair is *sīla-paññā*, with concentration as the technique that serves to cultivate both. Peter Harvey (*An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics*, 2000) instead confirms the classical threefold division into three distinct stages, *sīla-samādhi-paññā*. The two readings diverge in detail but agree on the essential point: the link is structural, not incidental.

Presenting *sīla* as "the moral rules of Buddhism," placed alongside meditation as one ingredient among others, is as misleading as presenting a house's foundation as "one architectural element among others." The primary sources do not treat it as an interchangeable ingredient, but as a causal condition. The Cetana Sutta, in fact, does not simply say "a foundation is needed": it describes, in order, how a solid foundation leads step by step to a house that stands, from the foundation to the walls to the roof. It is a sequence, not a list of interchangeable parts.
