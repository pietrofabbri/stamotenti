---
title: "Sīla (Buddhist Ethics)"
---

The foundation of a house is not the most visible or interesting part of the building, but without a stable foundation, the rest—the walls, the roof, and everything built on top of it—will not stand, or will stand much less securely. In Buddhism, *sīla* plays a similar role.

## Essential Definition

*Sīla* is the Pali word for “ethical conduct”: the set of principles that govern how we behave toward others and the world, in the Buddhist tradition. **It is not a rule imposed from the outside, disconnected from the rest of the practice: it is one of the three classic components of the Buddhist path, along with concentration (*samādhi*) and wisdom (*paññā*).**

## History of the Term

*Sīla* is the Pali form of the Sanskrit *śīla*. *Monier-Williams* lists a range of meanings for *śīla* that begins with the realm of habit and character—“habit, custom, way of life or conduct, nature, disposition”—and extends to the moral realm: “good disposition, moral conduct, integrity, virtue.” The ethical meaning thus arises from a broader usage—that of what a person habitually does and which shapes their character. In Buddhism, the term is included among the six or ten perfections (*pāramitā*) and serves as the first element of the three-part path described above. There is no known derivation from a verbal root accepted by the dictionaries consulted, and the word is best described based on its uses rather than its origin.

## In Context

The Pali Canon does not present *sīla* as mere advice, but as the first link in a causal chain. The Cetana Sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2) describes eleven sequential steps: ethical conduct produces the absence of remorse; from the absence of remorse arise joy and serenity; only then does mental concentration become possible, and from this, understanding. The Sonadanda Sutta (Dīgha-Nikāya 4) conveys the same idea with a more direct image: virtue and understanding “wash” one another, just as one hand washes the other.

Two seminal academic studies confirm this connection, though they describe it using slightly different models. Damien Keown (*The Nature of Buddhist Ethics*, 1992) argues that the truly inseparable pair is *sīla-paññā*, with concentration serving as the technique for cultivating both. Peter Harvey (*An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics*, 2000), on the other hand, confirms the classical three-part division into three distinct stages: *sīla-samādhi-paññā*. The two interpretations differ in the details, but agree on the essential point: the connection is structural, not incidental.

Presenting *sīla* as “the moral rules of Buddhism,” placed alongside meditation as just one ingredient among others, is as misleading as presenting the foundation of a house as “just one architectural element among others.” The primary sources do not treat it as an interchangeable ingredient, but as a causal condition. The Cetana Sutta, in fact, does not merely state, “Foundations are needed”; it describes, step by step, how a solid foundation gradually leads to a house that stands on its own—from the foundation to the walls to the roof. It is a sequence, not a list of interchangeable parts.
