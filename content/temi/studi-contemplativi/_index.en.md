To truly get to know a city, you need a surveyor’s map, municipal records, and the stories of those who live there. None of these three is sufficient on its own. Contemplative studies work in a similar way: they bring together different perspectives on the same practice.

## Essential Definition

**Contemplative Studies is an interdisciplinary field dedicated to research and education on contemplative practice and experience, including its potential applications in various contexts.** Louis Komjathy provides the first comprehensive introduction to the field (Komjathy 2017). By “contemplative practice,” he means a broad category that includes meditation, contemplative prayer, and related disciplines.

## History of the Term

“Contemplation” comes from the Latin *contemplatio*, from *contemplari*, composed of *con-* and *templum*. *Templum* did not originally refer to a building, but rather to a “carved out,” delimited space: the space in the sky or on the ground that the augur, the Roman soothsayer, demarcated in order to observe the signs (Online Etymology Dictionary). Regarding the origin of the word, scholarly literature proposes two hypotheses: an Indo-European root *\*temp-*, “to stretch,” or a formation parallel to the Greek *témenos*, “sacred enclosure,” from a root *\*temh₁-*, “to cut” (Walde and Hofmann, Pokorny, de Vaan, Ernout, and Meillet, cited in the corresponding Wiktionary entry). In both cases, the core meaning is that of a delimited space for observing.

The Greek world had a different family of words for the same activity. *Theōreîn* means “to look, to observe,” and from it come *theōría* and the adjective *theōrētikós*, “pertaining to observation and study,” from which “theory” and “theoretical” are derived. The two word families, the Latin one derived from *templum* and the Greek one from *theōreîn*, are formed on different bases in each language. That they corresponded in the scholarly usage of Greco-Roman culture is evidenced by the title of a treatise by Philo of Alexandria, a Greek-speaking Jewish philosopher, on the life of a community of ascetics: in Greek it is *Perì bíou theōrētikoú*, “On the Theoretical Life,” and in Latin it is known as *De vita contemplativa*, “On the Contemplative Life.”

As the name of an academic field, “Contemplative Studies” is very recent. According to Komjathy (2017), the field as such emerged in the 2000s, and Harold Roth of Brown University appears to have been the first to use the term (Roth 2006, 2008), within the framework of the Brown Contemplative Studies Initiative. When the International Symposium for Contemplative Studies was founded as part of the Mind & Life Institute, this name was preferred over “Contemplative Science.” The Contemplative Studies Group of the American Academy of Religion was founded in 2010.

The institutional context is broader. Morgan (2015) identifies three waves of interest in contemplative education in the United States: around 1840, between 1968 and 1974, and since 1995, the year the Center for Contemplative Mind in Society was founded.

## In Context

The field can adopt first-, second-, and third-person approaches. A distinctive feature is “first-person critical discourse”: personal practice can be used as a source of understanding, but it must remain subject to systematic and public scrutiny. Komjathy points out, however, that research can also be conducted in the third person, using historical, textual, and ethnographic methods. Alongside experimental and clinical methods, contemplative studies employ historical contextualization, literary analysis, and philosophical reflection.

The comparative spirit of the field is expressed in a statement by Harold Roth, cited by Komjathy: the study of “the many ways in which human beings have found, across cultures and throughout time, to focus, broaden, and deepen awareness” (Roth 2008, pp. 19–20, cited in Komjathy 2017; my translation).

Komjathy also expresses concern about the trivialization, commodification, and commercialization of contemplative practices when they are separated from the radical challenges of the traditions from which they originate.

In the text proposing the field, Roth (2006) outlines three objectives: to identify the varieties of contemplative experience, to provide scientific explanations for them, and to critically evaluate their significance, integrating the sciences, the humanities, and the arts.

Contemplative studies do not replace cultural history, sociology, or empirical psychology. Rather, they complement these fields: in particular, they provide a systematic comparison of different traditions, which other disciplines tend to treat separately.
