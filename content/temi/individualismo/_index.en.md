In an apartment building, there are those who take great care of their own apartment but never go up to check on the condition of the stairwell. No one reproaches them for it, but the stairwell remains everyone’s and no one’s.

## Essential Definition

**In the sense in which it has entered modern political and social discourse, “individualism” refers to an attitude: the tendency to withdraw into the close circle of family and friends, leaving the wider society to its own devices.** Alexis de Tocqueville describes it this way in the second volume of *Democracy in America* (1840), where he presents it as a recent term, linked to a new idea, and distinguishes it from selfishness: selfishness is an excessive love of oneself; individualism is a withdrawal from communal life.

## History of the Term

“Individual” comes from the Latin *individuus*, “indivisible,” formed with the negative prefix *in-* attached to *dividuus*, “divisible,” an adjective derived from the verb *dividere*. The verb is composed of *dis-*, “separately,” and an archaic element meaning “to separate,” which de Vaan traces back to a reconstructed Indo-European root *\*h₁weydʰh₁-*, “to divide.” The Latin *vidua*, “widow,” that is, a person separated from her spouse, is thought to derive from the same root.

In ancient usage, *individuus* does not refer to a person. Cicero uses it in *De finibus* (I, 17) to explain Epicurus’s physics: atoms are “bodies indivisible due to their solidity” (*corpora individua propter soliditatem*), which move through infinite space and come together upon colliding (my translation). *Individuus* renders the Greek *átomos*, “uncut,” reflecting its meaning: it denotes that which cannot be further divided.

“Individualism” is a much more recent term. According to Steven Lukes (1971), the first known use is by Joseph de Maistre, who in 1820 employed it in a negative sense regarding the fragmentation of doctrines: “political Protestantism taken to the most absolute individualism.” The Saint-Simonians, disciples of Saint-Simon, were the first to use it systematically in the mid-1820s.

In 1840, Tocqueville declared it a new term: “Individualism is a recent expression that a new idea has given rise to” (*De la démocratie en Amérique*, Part II, Chapter II; my translation). In another passage, cited by Lukes, he writes that our forefathers did not have the word “individualism,” which “we have coined for our own use” (translated here from the Italian; Lukes’s original English wording was not checked directly for this entry). In English, the philosophical and social meaning, as opposed to socialism, dates back to 1851, in the work of John Stuart Mill (Online Etymology Dictionary).

## In Context

Tocqueville does not consider it a constant of human nature. He links it to specific historical conditions: democratic equality, the end of stable aristocratic ties, and social mobility.

Throughout the nineteenth century, a cultural genre developed on this basis. In 1859, Samuel Smiles published *Self-Help*, the text that gave its name to the self-help genre, opening with the maxim “Heaven helps those who help themselves.” Nearly a century later, historian Irvin Wyllie demonstrated that the *self-made man*, the man who made himself without owing anything to anyone, was a myth of American culture rather than an accurate description of how society functioned. He traces the expression’s first appearance in American public discourse to 1832, in a speech by Senator Henry Clay, and identifies its antecedents in the sermons and moral literature of previous centuries.

Robert Bellah and his co-authors, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985, updated edition 1996), distinguish two forms of American individualism. The “hard” form is utilitarian and views economic success as a test of character. The “soft” form is expressive and is often experienced in a therapeutic context. The same authors observe that individualism has not entirely eliminated other moral traditions, such as the biblical and civic-republican traditions, which emphasize the social dimension of the person.

It is important not to confuse these concepts. Individualism, in the historical sense described here, is not synonymous with the value of personal autonomy. Radical self-sufficiency, that is, the idea that a mature person should not rely on others, is a more specific aspect of it, not a synonym.
