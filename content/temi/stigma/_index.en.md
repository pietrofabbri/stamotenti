title: "Stigma (public and internalized)"
---

A label sewn inside the collar is visible only to the person wearing that garment. Yet it can be enough to make that person avoid certain situations, even if no one else has ever noticed it.

## Essential Definition

**In the field of mental health, “stigma” refers to the negative social judgment associated with a mental illness or the use of psychological treatment.** Research distinguishes between public stigma, that is, the reaction of the general population, and self-stigma, that is, the prejudice a person directs toward themselves (Corrigan and Watson 2002). The latter is also referred to as internalized or self-directed stigma.

## History of the Term

“Stigma” comes from the Greek *stígma*, “mark, prick, brand,” derived from the verb *stízein*, “to prick, to brand.” Reconstructions trace it back to an Indo-European root *\*(s)teyg-*, “to prick, to point,” the same as the English *stick* (Online Etymology Dictionary; the reconstructed form is that given by Wiktionary). It passed into Latin as *stigma* (genitive *stigmatis*), “mark, stain, prick” (Treccani).

Erving Goffman, in the book that introduced the term into sociology, notes that the Greeks used it to refer to marks carved or burned onto the body, intended to make it clear that the bearer was a slave, a criminal, or a traitor: someone to be avoided, especially in public places. In the Greek text of the New Testament, the word appears only once, in the plural: in the Letter to the Galatians (6:17), Paul writes, “I bear in my body the *stígmata* of Jesus,” that is, the marks of Jesus. In the Christian era, according to Goffman, two layers of metaphor were added: the bodily signs of divine grace and, as a medical allusion to this religious allusion, the bodily signs of a physical disorder (Goffman 1963, chap. 1). For Goffman, in modern usage, stigma is an undesirable difference from the expectations of others, which separates those who bear it from those who do not, whom he calls “normal.”

## In Context

Sociology has provided a broad definition of the term. Bruce Link and Jo Phelan (2001) define stigma as the co-occurrence of labeling, stereotypes, separation, loss of status, and discrimination, and add that for stigmatization to occur, power must be exercised. Their framework is structural and does not rely on the distinction between public stigma and self-stigma.

This distinction, however, is central to research on mental health. Patrick Corrigan and Amy Watson (2002) describe stigma as a combination of stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination, and show how self-stigma, with the low self-esteem and diminished confidence in the future that it entails, leads people to give up on pursuing life opportunities.

With regard to seeking psychological help, David Vogel, Nathaniel Wade, and Shawn Haake (2006) developed a tool to measure self-stigma. They begin with the definition of stigma as the perception that those seeking psychological treatment are undesirable or socially unacceptable.

The relationship with culture is not linear. A study of four cultural groups in the United Kingdom, with a sample of 305 people, found higher levels of internalized stigma in the more collectivist groups, at least within that sample (Papadopoulos, Foster, and Caldwell 2013). A more recent meta-analysis, based on studies of patients diagnosed with psychosis, points in a similar direction: highly collectivist cultures are associated with higher levels of internalized stigma (Sum et al. 2024).

These findings have specific limitations. The first concerns a specific sample, and the second a specific clinical population. They do not allow us to conclude that it is generally more difficult to ask for help in collectivist cultures. However, they do reinforce the idea that cultural context matters: a context in which one depends on others can still impose a social cost on asking for help, distinct from that produced by the ideal of the self-sufficient individual.
