Imagine the word “sport”: running, weightlifting, and swimming are all sports, but they work different muscles in different ways. “Meditation” works the same way: it’s a category, not a single exercise.

Attention is like a muscle that can be trained through different exercises. Some strengthen it by keeping it focused on a single point, such as the breath. Others train it to notice when it wanders and to bring it back. Still others train it to remain open to everything that’s happening, without fixating on anything in particular.

## Essential Definition

**“Meditation” is an umbrella term for a broad set of diverse mental practices that train attention and awareness in an intentional and repeated manner. It is not a single technique: practices as diverse as observing the breath, repeating a word, walking mindfully, or contemplating an image all fall under the same name.** These practices originate in different traditions (Buddhist, Hindu, Christian contemplative, secular, or clinical) and have different purposes.

## Contextualizing the Term

Contemplative traditions have distinguished between different families of practice for centuries. A distinction also adopted by contemporary scientific research **separates “narrow-focus” practices—which concentrate attention on a single object such as the breath or a mantra—from “open-monitoring” practices, which remain aware of whatever arises without fixating on anything in particular.** This is the taxonomy proposed by Lutz, Slagter, Dunne, and Davidson (2008), which has become a standard reference in meditation studies. However, this is a useful simplification, not a perfect map: the *Oxford Handbook of Meditation* (Farias, Brazier, and Lalljee, eds., 2021) notes that many actual practices combine elements of both families, or do not fit neatly into either.

A common mistake in generalist treatments is to present meditation as if it were a single universal technique, such as “emptying the mind” or “relaxing.” This is not the case. Wallace (*Contemplative Science*, 2007) shows that **different traditions have very different models of what the mind does during practice and what the practice is intended to achieve.** Not all seek relaxation: some explicitly seek states of intense mental energy, while others seek specific philosophical insights.

From this broad category, two more specific concepts stand out, each discussed in a dedicated entry: ***mindfulness*, a particular family of meditative practices, and Buddhist meditation, from which contemporary clinical mindfulness derives** (see the entry on *MBSR*).

Shining a flashlight on a single object in a dark room is narrow focus. Turning on the light in the entire room, without focusing on anything in particular, is open monitoring. These are two different ways of “looking,” and they serve different purposes.
