The word “sport” encompasses running, weightlifting, and swimming, which work different muscles in different ways. “Meditation” works the same way: it’s a category, not a single exercise.

Attention is like a muscle that can be trained through different exercises. Some strengthen it by keeping it focused on a single point, such as the breath. Others train it to notice when it wanders and to bring it back. Still others train it to remain open to everything that’s happening, without fixating on anything in particular.

## Essential Definition

**“Meditation” is an umbrella term for a broad set of diverse mental practices that train attention and awareness in an intentional and repeated way. It is not a single technique: very different practices coexist under the same name, such as observing the breath, repeating a word, walking mindfully, or contemplating an image.** These practices originate in different traditions (Buddhist, Hindu, Christian contemplative, secular, or clinical) and have different purposes.

## History of the Term

“Meditate” comes from the Latin *meditari*, which, according to etymological dictionaries, is derived from the verb *medeor*, “to care for, to heal,” with a suffix expressing a repeated or intense action. *Medeor* traces back to a reconstructed Indo-European root *\*med-*, which means “to measure” and, by extension, “to judge, to give advice, to care for.” According to de Vaan (p. 368) and the *Lexikon der indogermanischen Verben* (p. 423), the meaning shifts from “to be one who judges” to “to be one who heals.” The same root also gives rise to *modus*, “measure, manner.” The verb denoting healing, the word denoting measure, and the word denoting meditation thus share the same root.

In the Greek world, the closest concept is *melétē*, “exercise, practice, care.” According to Pierre Hadot, who studied its history in ancient philosophy, *melétē* corresponds to the Latin *meditatio* and denotes a preparatory exercise. The best-known example is *melétē thanátou*, the practice of meditating on death, a precursor to the Latin and later Christian *meditatio mortis*.

In the Christian Middle Ages, the word had a specific place. Guigo II, a 12th-century Carthusian monk, in the *Scala claustralium* (“The Ladder of Monks”) organizes the contemplative life into four steps: reading (*lectio*), meditation (*meditatio*), prayer (*oratio*), and contemplation (*contemplatio*). He defines meditation as “the diligent activity of the mind which, guided by its own reason, seeks knowledge of a hidden truth” (*Meditatio est studiosa mentis actio, occultae veritatis notitiam ductu propriae rationis investigans*; my translation). In its original sense, the word thus refers to the mind’s active engagement with a text or a truth—a far cry from its current use as an umbrella term for various mindfulness practices.

## In Context

Contemplative traditions have distinguished between different families of practice for centuries. A distinction also adopted by contemporary scientific research separates “narrow-focus” practices—which concentrate attention on a single object such as the breath or a mantra—from “open-monitoring” practices, which remain aware of whatever arises without fixating on anything in particular. This is the taxonomy proposed by Lutz, Slagter, Dunne, and Davidson (2008), which has become a standard reference in meditation studies. However, this is a useful simplification, not a perfect map: the *Oxford Handbook of Meditation* (Farias, Brazier, and Lalljee, eds., 2021) notes that many actual practices combine elements of both families, or do not fit neatly into either.

A common mistake in generalist treatments is to present meditation as if it were a single universal technique, such as “emptying the mind” or “relaxing.” This is not the case. Wallace (*Contemplative Science*, 2007) shows that different traditions have very different models of what the mind does during practice and what the practice is intended to achieve. Not all seek relaxation: some explicitly seek states of intense mental energy, while others seek specific philosophical insights.

From this broad category, two more specific concepts stand out, each covered in a dedicated entry: *mindfulness*, a particular family of meditative practices, and Buddhist meditation, from which contemporary clinical mindfulness derives (see the entry on *MBSR*).

Shining a flashlight on a single object in a dark room is focused attention. Turning on the light to illuminate the entire room, without fixating on anything in particular, is open monitoring. These are two different ways of “looking,” and they serve different purposes.
