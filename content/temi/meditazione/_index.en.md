title: "Meditation"

Think of the word "sport": running, weightlifting, and swimming are all sports, but they train different muscles in different ways. "Meditation" works the same way: it is a category, not a single exercise.

Attention is like a muscle that can be trained through different exercises. Some strengthen it by holding it steady on one point, such as the breath. Others train it to notice when it wanders and to return. Others still train it to remain open to everything that happens, without fixing on anything in particular.

## Essential Definition

"Meditation" is an umbrella term for a broad set of mental practices, quite different from one another, that intentionally and repeatedly train attention and awareness. It is not a single technique: many very different practices share the same name, such as watching the breath, repeating a word, walking with attention, or contemplating an image. These practices arose in different traditions (Buddhist, Hindu, Christian contemplative, secular, or clinical) and serve different purposes.

## In Context

Contemplative traditions have distinguished different families of practice for centuries. A distinction also taken up by contemporary scientific research separates "focused-attention" practices, which concentrate attention on a single object such as the breath or a mantra, from "open-monitoring" practices, which remain aware of whatever arises without fixing on anything in particular. This is the taxonomy proposed by Lutz, Slagter, Dunne, and Davidson (2008), today a reference point in meditation research. It is, however, a useful simplification rather than a perfect map: the *Oxford Handbook of Meditation* (Farias, Brazier, and Lalljee, eds., 2021) notes that many real practices combine elements of both families, or do not fit well into either.

A common mistake in general-audience treatments is to present meditation as if it were a single universal technique — for instance, "emptying the mind" or "relaxing." This is not the case. Wallace (*Contemplative Science*, 2007) shows that different traditions hold very different models of what the mind does during practice and of what the practice is meant to achieve. Not all of them seek relaxation: some explicitly seek states of intense mental energy, others specific philosophical insights.

Two more specific things are distinguished from this broad category, each treated in its own entry: *mindfulness*, a particular family of meditative practices, and Buddhist meditation, from which contemporary clinical mindfulness derives (see the *MBSR* entry).

Shining a flashlight on a single object in a dark room is focused attention. Turning on the light for the whole room, without fixing on anything in particular, is open monitoring. These are two different ways of "looking," and they serve different purposes.
