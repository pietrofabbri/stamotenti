At the end of some articles, you’ll find a short guided exercise, usually three minutes or so. If you’re wondering whether it makes sense, or if it’s “real” meditation, this page attempts to answer that honestly.

## It’s Not a Structured Practice

Three minutes isn’t enough to build stable concentration, go through the stages of a formal practice, or achieve what you’d expect from a full-fledged meditation session—which usually lasts at least 15–20 minutes and follows a more structured progression. These exercises don’t claim to replace it: they’re a bridge, a way to bring a moment of mindful awareness into a part of the day where it wouldn’t otherwise be present, and—if you’re interested—an invitation to explore a more serious practice, whenever and if you’d like to.

## Does it actually make sense, or is it just a symbolic gesture?

Even very brief interventions have measurable effects: a 2018 study found that a single 10-minute meditation session improves attention in people who had never practiced before, with effects also visible in the brain’s electrical activity {{< cite "norris2018" >}}. Our exercises last less than those 10 minutes, and we don’t have specific data on exactly three minutes—we don’t want to overstate what we know. But the idea that “nothing happens below a certain threshold” isn’t supported by research: what changes with duration is how broad and stable the effect is, not whether it’s present or absent.

## Who Should Avoid Them

If you’re going through a period of intense emotional distress, these exercises aren’t designed for that, and simply pausing to observe difficult thoughts or feelings without more structured guidance may not be the right choice at that moment. In that case, the right course of action is to talk to a trained professional, not to do a three-minute exercise on your own.

## Who Reads the Practice

The voice you hear isn’t a synthetic one: I record it myself, with my own voice—the same person who writes the articles (see ["Who’s Behind It"](/en/il-progetto/)). Not all practices have an audio recording yet: when one is missing, the written text—with pauses indicated in parentheses—is still complete and can be used on its own, read aloud in a low voice or simply followed in silence, breath by breath.
