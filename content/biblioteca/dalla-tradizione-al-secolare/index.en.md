## The Missing Piece

In 1979, the scientist Jon Kabat-Zinn founded a program called MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction) at a university hospital in the United States {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}.

His goal was to adapt specific Buddhist meditation techniques and make them usable in a clinical setting for patients of any religion or none.

Since then, this form of meditation has spread widely: first in healthcare, then in schools and psychotherapy, and, over the past two decades, increasingly within corporations to help employees manage work-related stress.

Yet what changes when a religious and philosophical practice like Buddhist meditation is extracted from its original context and brought into a corporate environment?

The most consequential shift has nothing to do with the meditative technique itself, which remains largely unchanged. Rather, it concerns another dimension of the Buddhist tradition that is less visible but equally central: ethics {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}.

In traditional Buddhism, learning to observe one's own mind has never been separated from the question of how one acts toward others and the world.

Ethical conduct toward oneself and others establishes the conditions for a calmer, more focused mind. This mental focus, in turn, enables a clearer understanding of reality.

Conduct, attention, and understanding are three stages of a single path, rather than three independent elements that can be separated at will.

When meditation is framed primarily as a technique for reducing stress and improving workplace performance, as is often the case in corporate settings, this ethical dimension tends to disappear.

The scholar Ronald Purser argues that this omission is significant. When mindfulness is presented solely as an individual tool for stress management, it risks shifting attention away from the structural causes of workplace stress, such as excessive workloads, job insecurity, or poor organizational management, placing the burden instead on individual workers, who are expected to simply be more resilient {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}.

There is, however, an equally rigorous counter-perspective.

Kabat-Zinn has maintained that he deliberately adapted Buddhist meditation for a secular, medical setting, rather than distorting it by mistake or out of self-interest.

In the Buddhist tradition, this kind of deliberate adaptation is known as *upaya*, or "skillful means." It refers to conveying a teaching in the manner most beneficial to those receiving it, even by altering its external form, provided its essential core remains intact {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}.

From this perspective, Kabat-Zinn's approach is legitimate and well-founded, rather than a betrayal of tradition.

Corporate mindfulness is neither inherently false, useless, nor detrimental to those who practice it.

Nevertheless, removing the ethical framework represents a substantive change rather than a superficial simplification. It warrants close examination because mindfulness is no longer practiced solely in religious or therapeutic settings, but within organizations that actively shape the working lives and well-being of many people.

## Abstract

When Jon Kabat-Zinn established the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program in 1979, he described his approach as a deliberate application of "skillful means" (*upaya*): extracting mindfulness practices from their traditional Buddhist context to make them accessible within a secular clinical framework {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. Since then, MBSR has expanded across numerous medical settings, while secularized mindfulness has also entered the workplace. Research based on data from the National Health Interview Survey indicates that self-reported meditation practice among U.S. workers increased from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007; however, the study does not measure corporate adoption rates or program effectiveness {{< cite "kachan2017" >}}.

This article argues that the most significant change in this secular transition is not the loss of meditative technique, but rather the systematic removal of the ethical dimension (*sila*), which is intrinsically linked to contemplative practice in the Buddhist tradition {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. Canonical Pali literature articulates this relationship in two separate passages: the *Cetana Sutta* (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2) and the *Sonadanda Sutta* (Dīgha-Nikāya 4, D.i.123–124). Foundational monographs by {{< cite "keown1992" >}} and {{< cite "harvey2000" >}} treat this relationship as central to the ethical-contemplative framework. Furthermore, {{< cite "gethin2011" >}} identifies the philological consequence of secular translation as the loss of the ethical function of mindfulness, rather than merely the detachment of a religious context.

This article examines the critique formulated by Ronald Purser {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}} and Jamie Walsh {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}, who argue that removing the ethical framework can shift analytical focus away from structural causes of stress toward individual employee responsibility. At the same time, it evaluates Kabat-Zinn's position, which presents secularization as a conscious pedagogical choice while acknowledging the risks of mainstream diffusion {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The conclusion does not hold that secularized mindfulness is "false," but rather that its expansion into clinical and corporate spheres requires an examination of what is omitted and the potential implications of that omission.

## Introduction and Theoretical Framework

In 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn founded the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. His explicit objective was to make mindfulness practices derived from the Buddhist tradition accessible within a clinical, secular setting {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. MBSR and related interventions have since expanded beyond clinical environments into educational institutions, psychotherapy, and, over the last two decades, the corporate sector.

The sources reviewed here do not provide an independent, current estimate of the proportion of U.S. companies offering mindfulness programs. However, an empirical indication of contemplative practice among workers is available: an analysis of the National Health Interview Survey by {{< cite "kachan2017" >}} shows an increase in self-reported meditation from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007, based on a sample of 85,004 employed adults in the United States. These data reflect individual employee practices rather than employer-sponsored offerings, and they do not evaluate workplace outcomes.

This development fits within the broader phenomenon that David McMahan terms "modern Buddhism": not a simple transmission of tradition, but a historical encounter between Buddhist concepts and Western frameworks, including romanticism, scientific psychology, and therapeutic individualism {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}. Jeff Wilson presents a complementary view, characterizing the process as a "mutual transformation" between Buddhist meditation and American culture {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}. These interpretations address the same underlying phenomenon from different angles. However, a more specific analytical question remains: within this process of hybridization, what is set aside, and what consequences follow when the practice is integrated into a corporate environment?

In traditional Buddhist frameworks, meditative practice is inseparable from its ethical foundation. Within the Noble Eightfold Path (the eightfold framework through which the Buddhist tradition outlines the path to liberation), *sila* (ethical conduct) and *sati* (mindfulness) are described by Buddhist scholarship as structurally intertwined dimensions rather than modular or separable elements {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. Ethical conduct provides the necessary basis for contemplative development; it is not an ancillary component that can be discarded without altering the nature of the practice.

Critical scholarship on corporate mindfulness highlights this point of divergence from modern secular applications. Accordingly, this article investigates the argument that removing the ethical dimension transforms the functional role of the practice: shifting it from an integrated path of personal and relational transformation grounded in a moral framework toward an individual technique focused primarily on stress regulation and workplace productivity.

## Methodology

This study is not an original empirical investigation or a clinical review. It is a comparative historical-philosophical analysis synthesizing primary sources, critical literature, and published contextual quantitative data.

The primary sources include Kabat-Zinn's retrospective account of the origins of MBSR {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}} and his subsequent ethical reflection on the challenges of mainstreaming mindfulness {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The 2011 text outlines the founding of MBSR, its relationship to conventional medicine and mind-body research, and its explicit reliance on the concept of "skillful means."

In addition, the analysis draws on two canonical Pali sources: the *Cetana Sutta* (*Aṅguttara Nikāya* 11.2), which grounds the structural relationship between *sila* and *samadhi*/*sati*, and the *Sonadanda Sutta* (*Dīgha-Nikāya* 4, D.i.123–124). These texts are examined alongside foundational monographs by {{< cite "keown1992" >}} and {{< cite "harvey2000" >}}. Keown's *The Nature of Buddhist Ethics* is a 269-page study published in 1992, while Harvey's *An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics: Foundations, Values and Issues* was published by Cambridge University Press in 2000 (xx + 478 pp.).

The critical literature on corporate mindfulness is represented by Ronald Purser's general-audience work and his academic contributions on the topic {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}, complemented by {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}'s analysis applying Foucauldian concepts of biopower and neoliberal governmentality.

This theoretical analysis is situated within contextual data to ground the discussion without conflating institutional presence with clinical or organizational efficacy. First, organizational and historical figures concerning MBSR's expansion are drawn from the MBSR Collaborative, an industry source that does not represent independent academic verification {{< cite "mbsr-collaborative-history" >}}. Second, demographic data from the National Health Interview Survey, based on 85,004 employed U.S. adults, document the prevalence of practices such as meditation, yoga, tai chi, and qigong among workers {{< cite "kachan2017" >}}. Third, sociological findings from {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}} examine how mindfulness instructors in England and Wales across various sectors categorize their work across secular, religious, and spiritual lines.

{{< cite "nisbet2019" >}}'s estimate that approximately one in five U.S. companies offers mindfulness programs, representing a $1.5 billion annual market, is noted as an indicator of commercial growth in public discourse. However, it is not used as an independent benchmark of corporate adoption, as no direct peer-reviewed confirmation of these specific figures was identified in the literature reviewed. Consequently, these numbers are not treated as definitive quantitative evidence.

The broader historical-sociological context is supported by {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}, {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}, and {{< cite "giuliano2018" >}}, the latter focusing on what is lost during the intercultural transmission of mindfulness. Finally, {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}, published in the same issue of *Contemporary Buddhism* as Kabat-Zinn's account, offers a philological analysis of the ethical role of *sati* in early texts, contrasting it with modern clinical definitions.

This article does not attempt a clinical assessment of MBSR or a systematic review of corporate mindfulness outcomes. Data on institutional adoption, employee practice, or market size do not establish therapeutic, organizational, or social efficacy. The cited literature represents a targeted sample selected to evaluate key theoretical positions within the debate.

A paper by Monteiro on ethics in "value-neutral" therapies, identified during preliminary research, was excluded because no verifiable publication under that title could be confirmed.

## Results

### The Scale of the Phenomenon

Analyzing the transition from traditional Buddhist meditation to corporate mindfulness requires distinguishing among three distinct levels: corporate program offerings, employees' actual practice, and the broader commercial mindfulness market. These dimensions interact but are not equivalent.

Industry reporting by {{< cite "nisbet2019" >}} estimates that roughly one in five U.S. corporations provides some form of mindfulness program and places total sector revenues, encompassing mobile applications, training centers, courses, and organizational programs, at approximately $1.5 billion annually. While these figures reflect the prominence of mindfulness in commercial discourse, they do not constitute peer-reviewed measurements of enterprise adoption and must be interpreted with caution.

Independent empirical data are available regarding workers' self-reported practices. Analyzing National Health Interview Survey data from 2002, 2007, and 2012 across 85,004 employed U.S. adults, {{< cite "kachan2017" >}} found that reported meditation practice increased from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007. The study also examined yoga, tai chi, and qigong, identifying variations across occupational categories, with lower participation among agricultural and manual workers than among office workers. These data do not indicate the number of employer-sponsored programs, nor do they demonstrate changes in employee well-being or workplace conditions. They simply show that mindfulness-related practices had attained measurable prevalence among the U.S. workforce.

Similarly, reports that MBSR is offered in over 720 medical centers require contextualization. While published by the MBSR Collaborative to illustrate institutional reach, this figure stems from an industry body rather than an independent academic census. In this analysis, it is treated as an organizational estimate rather than a definitive measure of healthcare integration. The central theoretical argument does not depend on a single statistic; rather, it addresses how the translation of Buddhist practices into clinical and corporate domains can obscure or alter their original ethical framework.

### What Is Omitted, and Why

In Pali Buddhism, *sila* (ethics) and *sati* (mindfulness) are not modular components that can be disassembled and recombined without structural consequences. Philological scholarship shows that their relationship is fundamental to the Noble Eightfold Path {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}, and canonical literature models this through an explicit causal progression.

The *Cetana Sutta* (*Aṅguttara Nikāya* 11.2) outlines an eleven-step causal sequence initiated by ethical conduct. Virtue (*sila*) leads to freedom from remorse, which in turn fosters joy, rapture, bodily tranquility, and pleasure. Only through this progression does mental concentration (*samadhi*) emerge, providing the foundation for clear vision, disenchantment, detachment, and liberation. The sutta states that "skillful virtues have freedom from remorse as their purpose" and that mental qualities lead to subsequent qualities "for the sake of going from the near to the Further Shore" (*Cetana Sutta*, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu).

In this paradigm, *sila* is not an external moral injunction preceding practice; it is the causal prerequisite that makes concentration possible. Without it, remorse (*kukkucca*) persists, operating as one of the five hindrances (*nivarana*) to mental stability. Separating *sila* from contemplative practice disrupts the causal sequence upstream of meditative concentration.

Gethin provides a philological perspective consistent with this framework. In early Buddhist texts, *sati* operates as a form of "moral memory": in traditional mindfulness of breathing, the practitioner "is remembering that one should be trying to root out greed, hatred and delusion" {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}. Modern clinical definitions that center exclusively on "nonjudgmental" awareness can be problematic from a traditional Theravada standpoint (the Buddhist tradition, prominent in Southeast Asia, to which these Pali texts belong). The risk, Gethin observes, is the implication that all mental states possess equal validity, "that greed is as good as non-attachment" {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}. While the *Cetana Sutta* establishes the causal dependence of concentration on ethical conduct, Gethin demonstrates how the modern omission of the ethical function alters *sati* conceptually.

A second canonical source corroborates this structural interdependence. In the *Sonadanda Sutta* (*Dīgha-Nikāya* IV, D.i.123–124), the Brahmin Sonadanda illustrates the relationship between virtue (*sila*) and discernment (*pañña*) through the metaphor of hands washing one another: "understanding is washed around by virtue, and virtue is washed around by understanding […] just as one hand might wash the other or one foot might wash the other" {{< cite "keown1992|38–39" >}}, a formulation affirmed by the Buddha.

Keown places this dynamic at the core of his analysis. His thesis clarifies the structural relationship: the fundamental, inseparable pair is *sila-pañña*, whereas *samadhi* functions as the meditative vehicle through which both are cultivated, rather than an independent third component of equal standing {{< cite "keown1992|111–121" >}}. Harvey similarly confirms both the standard threefold division of the Path (*sila-samadhi-pañña*) and the canonical teachings to Ānanda supporting the model found in the *Cetana Sutta* {{< cite "harvey2000|37–41" >}}. Both monographs emphasize that this structural cohesion is central to the traditional ethical-contemplative architecture, whether modeled through twofold or threefold frameworks.

### As Described by Kabat-Zinn

Kabat-Zinn does not describe the extraction of mindfulness from its Buddhist origins as an accidental omission or an inadvertent simplification. He frames it as a deliberate strategy grounded in the Buddhist principle of *upaya*—the "skillful means" by which teachings are adapted to the capacities and contexts of their audience {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}.

In his historical account of MBSR, Kabat-Zinn emphasizes the integration of the program with conventional medicine and mind-body research, noting the care required when translating traditional frameworks into secular terminology.

Nearly four decades later, Kabat-Zinn revisited this transition, addressing the ethical and practical challenges associated with the mainstream dissemination of the Dharma across medicine, commerce, education, politics, and social justice {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The ethical concerns raised by contemporary critics are thus not absent from his own reflections. However, he approaches them from a different perspective: viewing secular adaptation as necessary for establishing clinical and institutional legitimacy, while acknowledging the tensions generated by widespread popularization.

### The Critical Reading

Ronald Purser argues that detaching the ethical framework when importing mindfulness into corporate settings can shift attention away from systemic drivers of stress, such as workload, job insecurity, and organizational practices, recasting stress management as an individual employee responsibility {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}.

Jamie Walsh advances a compatible critique using Foucault's concept of biopower, suggesting that corporate mindfulness functions as an instrument of employee self-regulation rather than an avenue for liberation from suffering {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}.

These analyses offer theoretical critiques of how mindfulness operates within organizational systems. The literature reviewed here does not empirically establish that all workplace mindfulness initiatives lead to individualization, passive adaptation, or increased organizational compliance; nor does it demonstrate that such outcomes never occur. Rather, it highlights the theoretical and political risk that arises when contemplative practices are repurposed within frameworks centered on performance, resilience, and productivity, potentially obscuring structural working conditions in favor of individual adaptation.

The prevalence of meditation among workers and the commercial scale of the mindfulness sector justify critical examination of these dynamics. However, market size and individual practice do not constitute evidence of specific organizational outcomes. Establishing the concrete effects of corporate programs on well-being, stress, absenteeism, organizational culture, or employee perceptions would require dedicated, comparative empirical research within workplace settings.

### How This Tension Is Experienced

A study by Arat and Hemming provides insight into how these tensions are navigated by practitioners who teach mindfulness. Covering diverse sectors in England and Wales, including healthcare, education, business, governance, and religious organizations, the study explores how instructors locate their work relative to secular, religious, and spiritual categories {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}}.

Because the full text of the study was unavailable for this review, specific quantitative breakdowns and self-identification percentages are not reported. Nonetheless, the existence of a major sociological investigation into how teachers conceptualize the boundaries between secularity, religion, and spirituality illustrates the live nature of these debates within the field.

### A Broader Historical Framework

Corporate mindfulness can be situated within the historical framework described by McMahan, in which Western cultural assumptions, particularly therapeutic individualism and economic instrumentalism, reshape secularized Buddhist practices {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}.

Giuliano examines what is lost during this intercultural transmission, resisting simple binaries of institutional success versus spiritual decline {{< cite "giuliano2018" >}}. Concurrently, Wilson cautions against viewing this evolution solely through a lens of loss or subtraction. In his analysis, secular mindfulness is not merely an impoverished variant of traditional practice, but the outcome of a complex cultural exchange with modern Western society {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}. This perspective does not invalidate ethical critiques; rather, it highlights the multifaceted nature of cultural adaptation.

## Discussion

The evidence examined does not support the conclusion that corporate mindfulness is simply a "false" distortion of an "authentic" Buddhism. Both McMahan and Wilson caution against static views of religious traditions, emphasizing that living practices evolve over time and that authenticity remains an elusive analytical standard.

The literature does, however, support a more precise structural observation: the reduction or systematic omission of the ethical framework represents a substantive transformation in the secularization of mindfulness. Canonical texts, contemporary philology, and the monographs of Keown and Harvey all describe an integral relationship connecting ethical conduct, meditative attention, concentration, and insight. When this integrated framework is replaced by a practice defined primarily around attentional training, stress reduction, or emotional self-regulation, the underlying logic and purpose of the practice are fundamentally altered.

Purser and Walsh's critique should not be interpreted as empirical proof of the outcomes of all corporate initiatives. It represents a normative, historical, and organizational critique, highlighting how practices may be instrumentalized within institutions and the conceptual shifts that occur when stress is framed as an individual deficit. Rigorously testing these arguments requires empirical research comparing diverse program designs, organizational environments, and labor conditions.

The quantitative data cited here have a defined scope. Industry estimates demonstrate the commercial visibility of mindfulness, while NHIS survey data confirm that contemplative practices were already utilized by a segment of the U.S. workforce. These data do not demonstrate clinical efficacy, nor do they prove that workplace mindfulness either reinforces or mitigates the structural drivers of workplace stress.

Kabat-Zinn's perspective must be understood within this context. He conceptualizes secularization as an intentional adaptation and explicitly notes the risks associated with the mainstream diffusion of the Dharma. While this does not resolve the critique advanced by scholars such as Purser or Walsh, it demonstrates that ethical awareness is not solely external to secular mindfulness discourse.

## Conclusions

The analysis presented here rests on three central findings. First, Buddhist philology identifies *sila* and *sati* as interdependent dimensions that cannot be detached without fundamentally altering the nature of the path {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. Second, Kabat-Zinn has acknowledged the ethical challenges inherent in adapting mindfulness outside its traditional context {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. Third, mindfulness practices have established a significant presence within clinical, workplace, and commercial domains, though existing metrics capture distinct facets of adoption and must not be conflated with empirical proof of organizational or therapeutic efficacy.

The most defensible conclusion is not that corporate mindfulness lacks utility or constitutes a corrupted practice, but that the removal of its ethical architecture represents a substantive change in how the practice is structured and applied. This shift warrants critical attention because mindfulness now operates within institutional environments that directly influence the working conditions, health, and well-being of large populations.

Whether this structural change is permanent remains an open question. Wilson's model of cultural exchange suggests that modern adaptations could eventually incorporate new ways of engaging ethical concerns. However, current research does not provide sufficient empirical basis to determine whether such reintegration is occurring within corporate settings, or what outcomes it might produce.

## Limitations

This article relies on a targeted selection of sources designed to evaluate key perspectives in the literature. It does not assess the clinical efficacy of MBSR, which requires dedicated clinical trials and cannot be derived from institutional adoption figures, employee surveys, or industry market data.

The figures cited from {{< cite "nisbet2019" >}}, estimating that one in five U.S. companies offers mindfulness programs within a $1.5 billion industry, represent trade estimates reported in public discourse. They illustrate the perceived commercial scope of the sector but are not treated here as verified empirical measures of enterprise adoption. Similarly, the MBSR Collaborative's report of adoption across more than 720 medical centers originates from an affiliated organization and should be understood as an organizational estimate rather than an independent academic census.

The analysis by {{< cite "kachan2017" >}} provides stronger empirical footing through its use of the National Health Interview Survey and a sample of 85,004 employed adults. However, it measures general self-reported participation in meditation, yoga, tai chi, and qigong; it does not track employer-provided programs, isolate dedicated workplace mindfulness interventions, or evaluate workplace outcomes.

Regarding {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}}, limited access to the full text prevented the inclusion of detailed quantitative distributions. Reference to this study is restricted to its scope and research focus rather than its empirical findings. Future reviews should incorporate the full dataset when available.

This review incorporates two primary canonical Pali sources (*Cetana Sutta* and *Sonadanda Sutta*), a philological analysis of the ethical dimensions of *sati* {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}, and two foundational monographs {{< cite "keown1992" "harvey2000" >}}. These sources were consulted for passages specifically addressing the theoretical core of this study, rather than through an exhaustive analysis of their complete texts.

Direct verification of citations on pages 38–39 and 111–121 of Keown and pages 37–41 of Harvey remains necessary, alongside the standardization of all bibliographic entries according to a single academic citation format. While the publication details, years, and overarching arguments of both monographs align with standard bibliographic records, confirming specific page citations requires direct inspection of the physical editions cited.
