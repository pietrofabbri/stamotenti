In 1979, scientist Jon Kabat-Zinn founded a program called [MBSR](/temi/mbsr/)—Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction—at a university hospital in the United States {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. His goal was to take certain Buddhist [meditation](/temi/meditazione/) techniques and adapt them for use in a hospital setting, for patients of any religion or none at all.

Since then, this type of meditation has spread widely: first in the medical field, then in schools, in psychotherapy, and, over the past twenty years, increasingly in the workplace, to help people manage work-related stress. **But what changes when a religious and philosophical practice like Buddhist meditation is taken out of its original context and introduced into the workplace?**

The most significant change does not concern the meditation technique itself, which remains more or less the same. **It concerns another aspect of the Buddhist tradition, one that is less visible but just as central: ethics ([sila](/temi/sila/))** {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. In traditional Buddhism, learning to observe one’s own mind has never been separated from the question of how one behaves toward others and the world: proper conduct toward oneself and others creates the conditions for a calmer and more focused mind, and this focus, in turn, allows for a better understanding of things. Behavior, mindfulness, and understanding are three stages of the same journey, not three independent elements that can be separated at will.

When meditation is presented primarily as a technique for reducing stress and improving work performance—as is often the case in corporate courses—this ethical dimension tends to disappear. Researcher Ronald Purser argues that this is no minor detail: if [mindfulness](/temi/mindfulness/) is presented solely as a personal tool for managing stress, **there is a risk of shifting the focus from the true causes of workplace stress—such as excessive workloads, job insecurity, or poor organization—to the responsibility of the individual worker**, who “should” simply be more resilient {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}.

However, there is also an opposing viewpoint, one that is equally serious. Kabat-Zinn himself has always openly stated that he consciously adapted Buddhist meditation for a medical and secular context, not that he distorted it by mistake or out of self-interest. In the Buddhist tradition, this type of conscious adaptation has a name: **[upaya](/temi/upaya/), “skillful means”—teaching something in the way that is most useful to the recipient, even by changing the form, as long as the substance remains valid** {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. From this perspective, Kabat-Zinn’s choice is legitimate and well-founded, not a betrayal of tradition.

Corporate mindfulness is not false, useless, or harmful to those who practice it. But **the removal of the ethical component is a real change, not just a superficial simplification, and deserves attention**: today, mindfulness is no longer practiced only in religious or therapeutic contexts, but also in companies that influence the work and well-being of a great many people.

## Abstract

When Jon Kabat-Zinn founded the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program, MBSR, in 1979, he described his approach as a conscious and deliberate choice of “skillful means” (upaya): extracting mindfulness practices from their Buddhist context to make them accessible in a clinical and secular setting {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. Since then, MBSR has spread to many medical and clinical settings, while secularized mindfulness has also extended into the workplace. Research based on data from the National Health Interview Survey shows that, in the United States, the reported practice of meditation among workers rose from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007; however, the study does not measure how many employers offer mindfulness programs, nor the effectiveness of such programs {{< cite "kachan2017" >}}.

**This article argues that the most significant change in this transition does not concern the loss of the meditative technique, but rather the systematic removal of the ethical dimension (sīla), which in the Buddhist tradition is linked to contemplative practice** {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. The canon articulates this relationship in two separate passages, the Cetana Sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2) and the Sonadanda Sutta (Dīgha-Nikāya 4, D.i.123–124). Two seminal monographs, {{< cite "keown1992" >}} and {{< cite "harvey2000" >}}, treat this relationship as a central element of the ethical-contemplative framework. {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}, for its part, describes the philological consequence of the secular translation as the loss of an ethical function of mindfulness, not merely within a religious context.

The article takes seriously the criticism of Ronald Purser {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}} and Jamie Walsh {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}, according to whom the removal of the ethical framework can shift the focus from the structural causes of stress to the responsibility of the individual worker. However, it also considers the position of Kabat-Zinn, who presents secularization as a deliberate choice and has acknowledged the risks associated with the mainstream spread of mindfulness {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The conclusion is not that secularized mindfulness is “false,” but that the spread of mindfulness in clinical and corporate settings makes it necessary to ask what is being removed and what consequences this removal may have.

## Introduction and Theoretical Framework

In 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn founded the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. His stated goal was to make mindfulness practices derived from the Buddhist tradition accessible in a clinical and secular context {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}. Since then, MBSR and related practices have expanded beyond the clinical setting, entering schools, psychotherapy, and, over the past two decades, the workplace.

The sources used here do not provide an independent, up-to-date estimate of the proportion of U.S. companies offering mindfulness programs. However, there is an independent indication of the prevalence of contemplative practices among workers: an analysis of data from the National Health Interview Survey conducted by {{< cite "kachan2017" >}} reveals an increase in self-reported meditation from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007, based on a total sample of 85,004 employed adults in the United States. This data pertains to workers’ practices, not corporate offerings of courses, and does not measure the effects of mindfulness on the job.

This trend is part of a broader phenomenon that David McMahan has termed “modern Buddhism”: not a simple transfer of tradition, but a historic encounter between Buddhist elements and Western concepts, including romanticism, scientific psychology, and therapeutic individualism {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}. Jeff Wilson offers a complementary interpretation, speaking of a “mutual transformation” between Buddhist meditation and American culture {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}. The two interpretations can coexist: they describe the same process from different perspectives. However, a more specific question remains: in the process of hybridization, what is left behind, and what effects does this selection produce when the practice enters a corporate context?

In the traditional Buddhist context, meditative practice is not separate from the ethical component. In the Noble Eightfold Path—the sequence of eight elements through which the Buddhist tradition describes the path to liberation—sila and sati are presented by Buddhist scholarship as dimensions intertwined within the very structure of the path, not as independent or separable elements {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. Ethical conduct constitutes the foundation upon which contemplative practice develops; it is not a secondary requirement that can be eliminated while leaving the rest unchanged.

Critical literature on corporate mindfulness identifies an important difference here compared to contemporary secular applications. **This article therefore examines the thesis that the removal of the ethical dimension alters the function of the practice: from a path of personal and relational transformation, embedded in a moral framework, to an individual tool aimed primarily at stress management and, in the corporate context, at productivity.**

## Methodology

This text is neither an original empirical study nor a clinical review. It is a comparative historical-philosophical analysis that draws connections between primary sources, critical literature, and contextual quantitative data published elsewhere.

The primary sources include, on the one hand, Kabat-Zinn’s description of his founding work in 2011 and a subsequent, explicitly ethical reflection on the risks of mindfulness’s mainstream diffusion {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The 2011 article traces the origins of MBSR, its relationship with traditional medicine and mind-body science, and explicitly addresses the theme of “skillful means” (skillful means).

On the other hand, the text considers two canonical Pali sources: the Cetana Sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2), used to ground the question of the relationship between sila and samadhi/sati directly in the canon, and the Sonadanda Sutta (Dīgha-Nikāya 4, D.i.123–124). These passages are read in conjunction with the monographs by Keown (1992) and Harvey (2000), which were consulted directly in this review. Keown’s *The Nature of Buddhist Ethics* is a 269-page monograph published in 1992; Harvey’s *An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics: Foundations, Values, and Issues* was published by Cambridge University Press in 2000 and consists of xx + 478 pages.

The critical literature on corporate mindfulness is represented by Ronald Purser, in his best-known popular work and in an academic contribution dedicated to critical perspectives on corporate mindfulness {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}. This reading is complemented by that of Jamie Walsh {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}, who interprets the phenomenon through Foucaultian concepts of biopower and neoliberal governmentality.

This framework is supplemented by contextual data, used to situate the topic within a verifiable framework without mistaking it for evidence of mindfulness’s effectiveness. The first consists of historical and organizational data on the spread of MBSR reported by the MBSR Collaborative, an industry source that should not be treated as independent verification {{< cite "mbsr-collaborative-history" >}}. The second consists of a study based on the National Health Interview Survey, conducted among 85,004 employed adults in the United States and focusing on the frequency of practices such as meditation, yoga, tai chi, and qigong among workers {{< cite "kachan2017" >}}. The third is the sociological project by Arat and Hemming, conducted in England and Wales since 2017 across multiple sectors, on how mindfulness teachers position themselves in relation to the categories of secular, religious, and spiritual {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}}.

The estimate from {{< cite "nisbet2019" >}}—which states that approximately one in five U.S. companies offers mindfulness programs and that the sector generates $1.5 billion annually—is cited as an indicator of the journalistic and commercial debate surrounding the sector’s growth. However, it is not used as independent data to quantify corporate adoption. The research examined here did not find an equivalent peer-reviewed public source that directly confirms those two figures. To avoid attributing an excessive degree of certainty to them, they are not placed at the center of the quantitative discussion.

The historical-sociological framework is complemented by {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}, {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}, and {{< cite "giuliano2018" >}}, the latter devoted specifically to the question of what is lost in the intercultural transmission of mindfulness. {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}, published in the same issue of *Contemporary Buddhism* that features the account by Kabat-Zinn cited here, provides instead a philological analysis of the ethical function of *sati* in ancient texts, in contrast to contemporary clinical definitions.

An evaluation of the clinical efficacy of MBSR and a systematic review of the literature on corporate mindfulness fall outside the scope of this article. Data on institutional presence, practices reported by employees, or the commercial dimensions of the sector do not demonstrate the therapeutic, organizational, or social efficacy of mindfulness. The sources used constitute a targeted, non-exhaustive sample, selected to represent relevant positions in the debate.

A report by Monteiro on ethics in “value-neutral” therapies, identified during the research, was not included because no verifiable publication with the same title could be found.

## Results

### The Scale of the Phenomenon

Before discussing what changes in the transition from Buddhist meditation to corporate mindfulness, it is necessary to distinguish between three levels: the provision of programs by companies, the actual practice of employees, and the commercial market for mindfulness. These levels are interconnected but do not coincide.

An industry news source, {{< cite "nisbet2019" >}}, estimates that about one in five companies in the United States offers some form of mindfulness program and puts the sector’s revenue—which includes apps, centers, courses, and corporate programs—at around $1.5 billion per year. These figures show how mindfulness has been portrayed as a broad commercial and organizational phenomenon. However, they do not constitute an independent, peer-reviewed measure of corporate adoption and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

One independent data point, however, concerns practices reported by workers. {{< cite "kachan2017" >}}, analyzing data from the National Health Interview Survey for 2002, 2007, and 2012 on 85,004 employed adults in the United States, found that self-reported meditation practice rose from 8.0% in 2002 to 9.9% in 2007. The study also considers yoga, tai chi, and qigong, and observes significant differences among occupational groups: the practice is less frequent among agricultural and manual laborers than among office workers. The data do not indicate how many programs are offered by employers, nor whether such programs improve well-being or working conditions. It merely indicates that practices considered mindfulness-based were already present to a significant extent among the U.S. working population.

The figure stating that MBSR is offered at over 720 medical centers also requires qualification. The MBSR Collaborative presents this as information on the program’s reach, but the source is not an independent academic survey. In this article, the figure is therefore treated as an organizational estimate, not as a definitive measure of MBSR’s presence in healthcare. **The theoretical issue addressed in this article does not depend on the accuracy of a single figure: it concerns the transfer of Buddhist-derived practices into clinical, therapeutic, and corporate contexts where the original ethical framework may be transformed or rendered less visible.**

### What Is Omitted, and Why

In Pali Buddhism, sila and sati are not independent components that can be freely broken down and recombined. Philological literature describes their relationship as structural within the Noble Eightfold Path {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. The canon makes this relationship explicit through a causal sequence.

The Cetana Sutta (Aṅguttara Nikāya 11.2) presents a chain of eleven steps beginning with ethical conduct. Virtue (sila) produces the absence of remorse; from the absence of remorse arise joy, rapture, bodily serenity, and pleasure. Only then does mental concentration (samadhi) arise, upon which clear vision of reality, disenchantment, detachment, and liberation are founded. The text states that “skillful virtues have freedom from remorse as their purpose” and that mental qualities lead to other mental qualities “for the sake of going from the near to the Further Shore” (Cetana Sutta, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu).

In this framework, sila does not precede practice as an external moral norm. It is the first causal condition that makes concentration possible. In its absence, remorse (kukkucca) does not cease; in the traditional classification, remorse is counted among the five hindrances (nivarana) to the stabilization of the mind. **Separating sila from contemplative practice thus breaks a chain that lies upstream of concentration.**

Gethin proposes a philological interpretation consistent with this framework. In the ancient texts, sati functions as a form of “moral memory”: in the traditional practice of mindfulness of breathing, the practitioner “is remembering that one should be trying to root out greed, hatred and delusion” {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}. Contemporary clinical definitions, when they emphasize “nonjudgmental” observation, may appear problematic from a traditional Theravada perspective (the Buddhist school, widespread primarily in Southeast Asia, to which the Pali texts cited here belong). The risk, Gethin observes, is that this implies all mental states are of equal value, “that greed is as good as non-attachment” {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}. The Cetana Sutta sets forth the causal relationship between ethical conduct and concentration; Gethin shows what changes conceptually when the ethical function of sati disappears from the modern definition.

A second canonical passage, independent of the Cetana Sutta, confirms that the issue does not depend on a single textual formulation. In the Sonadanda Sutta (Dīgha-Nikāya IV, D.i.123–124), the Brahmin Sonadanda describes the relationship between virtue (sīla) and insight (pañña) using the image of one hand washing the other: “understanding is washed around by virtue, and virtue is washed around by understanding […] just as one hand might wash the other or one foot might wash the other” {{< cite "keown1992|38-39" >}}. The Buddha confirms this description.

Keown devotes a central part of his study to this connection. His thesis clarifies the interpretation proposed here without contradicting it: the structurally inseparable pair would properly be sila-pañña, while samadhi would constitute a technique that enables the cultivation of both, not a fully independent third term on the same level {{< cite "keown1992|111-121" >}}. Harvey, from an independent perspective, confirms both the standard three-part division of the Noble Eightfold Path into sila-samadhi-pañña and the passage addressed to Ānanda that supports the interpretation of the Cetana Sutta {{< cite "harvey2000|37-41" >}}. The two monographs thus treat the relationship between these dimensions as a cornerstone of the ethical-contemplative framework, even while describing its architecture using two- or three-term models.

### As Described by Kabat-Zinn

Kabat-Zinn does not describe the extraction of mindfulness from its Buddhist context as an accident or an unwitting simplification. **He presents it as a deliberate choice, rooted in the Buddhist concept of upaya: the “skillful means” by which a teaching is adapted to the context and the capacities of those receiving it** {{< cite "kabatzinn2011" >}}.

In his article on the origins of MBSR, Kabat-Zinn highlights the program’s connection to conventional medicine and to research on the mind-body relationship. He also emphasizes the need to handle with care the conceptual frameworks used to present the practice outside its original context.

Nearly forty years later, Kabat-Zinn revisited the topic in a text dedicated to the challenges and risks—ethical and otherwise—of the mainstream dissemination of the Dharma in contexts such as medicine, business, education, politics, and social justice {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The ethical concerns raised by critics are therefore not foreign to his thinking. He addresses them, however, from a different perspective: he considers the secular adaptation necessary to make these practices available in clinical and public settings, without ignoring the tensions created by their widespread circulation.

### The Critical Perspective

Ronald Purser argues that the removal of the ethical dimension, when mindfulness is transferred to the corporate context, can shift the focus from the collective causes of workplace stress—such as workloads, job insecurity, and work organization—to the individual worker’s responsibility for managing their own response to such conditions {{< cite "purser2019" "purser2018" >}}.

Jamie Walsh proposes a similar interpretation, but one grounded in Foucault’s concept of biopower. Corporate mindfulness can be interpreted as a tool for the self-regulation of workers, rather than as a path to liberation from suffering {{< cite "walsh2018" >}}.

These are critical interpretations of the role that mindfulness can play in organizations. The sources considered in this article do not empirically demonstrate that all corporate mindfulness programs lead to the individualization of stress, passive adaptation, or greater worker compliance. Nor do they allow us to conclude that such effects are absent. **Rather, they show that when the practice is embedded in a framework centered on performance, resilience, or productivity, there is a theoretical and political risk of shifting the focus from working conditions to the individual’s adaptive capacities.**

The spread of meditation practices among workers and the commercial visibility of the sector make this critique worthy of examination. However, these factors alone do not transform it into causal evidence. To determine the effects of workplace programs on well-being, stress, absenteeism, work relationships, or perceptions of organizational responsibilities, specific, comparative, and workplace-based empirical studies would be necessary.

### How This Tension Is Experienced

A project by Arat and Hemming helps determine whether this tension is also present among those who teach mindfulness. The large-scale study, conducted in England and Wales and spanning multiple sectors—including healthcare, education, business, politics, and religion—examines how teachers position themselves in relation to the categories of secular, religious, and spiritual {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}}.

The full text of the study was not available for this draft. For this reason, no self-identification percentages or specific quantitative results are reported. It remains relevant to the topic, however, that a sociological project of this scope has focused on teachers’ positions regarding the relationship between secularism, religion, and spirituality.

### A Broader Historical Framework

The phenomenon of corporate mindfulness can be situated within the broader process described by McMahan. In this process, Western categories—in this case, therapeutic individualism and the logic of productivity—influence the form of secularized Buddhist practice {{< cite "mcmahan2008" >}}.

Giuliano directly addresses the question of what may be lost in the intercultural transmission of mindfulness, without reducing the phenomenon to a simple opposition between success and decline {{< cite "giuliano2018" >}}. Wilson, on the other hand, proposes that the entire process not be interpreted solely as a process of subtraction. **Secular mindfulness is not simply an impoverished form of the traditional one, but the result of an exchange with the culture that received it** {{< cite "wilson2014" >}}. This perspective does not eliminate the criticism regarding the ethical dimension; rather, it makes its evaluation more complex.

## Discussion

The sources examined do not allow us to conclude that corporate mindfulness is “false” compared to an “authentic” form of Buddhism. McMahan and Wilson both caution against a static conception of religious traditions: living traditions change over time, and the concept of authenticity is difficult to apply as a simple criterion.

The sources do, however, allow us to make a more precise observation. **The reduction or explicit elimination of the ethical dimension is a significant change in the process of the secularization of mindfulness.** Canonical texts, modern philology, and the monographs by Keown and Harvey describe a close relationship between ethical conduct, meditative attention, concentration, and understanding. When this relationship is replaced by a practice defined primarily in terms of attention, stress reduction, or self-regulation, the framework within which the practice is presented and understood changes.

Purser and Walsh’s critique should not be treated as conclusive empirical evidence regarding the effects of all corporate programs. It is a normative, historical, and political critique: it draws attention to the way in which a practice can be used within an organization and to the consequences that may arise if stress is defined primarily as an individual problem. To empirically test this hypothesis, research would be needed that compares different types of programs, different organizational cultures, and different working conditions.

The quantitative data cited in this article serve a more limited purpose. Industry estimates indicate that mindfulness has achieved significant commercial visibility; NHIS data show that practices such as meditation were already widespread among a segment of the U.S. workforce. None of this data demonstrates the clinical efficacy of mindfulness, nor does it prove that workplace mindfulness either reinforces or reduces the structural causes of stress.

Kabat-Zinn’s position should also be situated within this distinction. Kabat-Zinn presents secularization as a deliberate adaptation and draws attention to the risks accompanying the mainstream dissemination of the Dharma. This position does not eliminate the disagreement with Purser or Walsh, but it shows that the ethical issue does not arise solely from criticism external to secular mindfulness.

## Conclusions

The argument of this article rests on three interconnected elements. The first is that Buddhist philology describes sila and sati as interdependent dimensions, not as components that can be separated without altering the path {{< cite "stanley2015" >}}. The second is that Kabat-Zinn has acknowledged the existence of ethical risks in the dissemination of mindfulness outside its original context {{< cite "kabatzinn2017" >}}. The third is that meditation and mindfulness practices have gained prominence in healthcare, workplace, and commercial contexts, although the available figures measure different aspects of the phenomenon and should not be confused with evidence of efficacy or causal impact.

The most prudent conclusion is not that corporate mindfulness is without value, nor that it is a corrupted version of Buddhism. **It is that the removal of the ethical component constitutes a substantial change in the way the practice is defined and employed.** This change warrants public attention because mindfulness no longer circulates solely within limited religious or therapeutic contexts but also within organizations that influence the work, health, and well-being of many people.

The question of whether this change is irreversible remains open. Wilson’s analysis leaves room for the possibility that the exchange between Buddhist tradition and the receiving culture may produce new forms of reintegrating the ethical dimension. However, the sources considered here do not allow us to determine whether this is already happening in corporate programs, nor what the results might be.

## Limitations

This article uses a sample of sources selected to represent key positions in the debate. It does not address the clinical efficacy of MBSR, which requires specific clinical studies and cannot be inferred from data on institutional adoption, practices reported by employees, or industry-wide commercial estimates.

The figure of {{< cite "nisbet2019" >}}—referring to one in five U.S. companies with annual revenues of $1.5 billion—is a journalistic estimate based on industry data. It is useful for documenting how the growth of mindfulness has been portrayed in public discourse, but it is not treated here as an independent and definitive measure of corporate adoption. The figure from the MBSR Collaborative regarding 720 or more medical centers has the same limitation: it comes from an organization linked to MBSR training and should be interpreted as an organizational estimate.

{{< cite "kachan2017" >}} provide more robust data because they use the National Health Interview Survey and a sample of 85,004 employed adults. Their study, however, measures self-reported practice of meditation, yoga, tai chi, and qigong among workers. It does not measure the availability of programs offered by employers, does not isolate workplace mindfulness, and does not assess the effects of the programs on working conditions.

For the study by {{< cite "arat-hemming2025" >}}, limited access to the full text prevented the reporting of specific quantitative results. The citation concerns the existence and scope of the project, not its empirical conclusions. A subsequent review should obtain the full text and incorporate the available data.

This revision adds two independent primary canonical sources, the Cetana Sutta and the Sonadanda Sutta, a more direct philological reading of the ethical function of sati {{< cite "gethin2011" >}}, and two reference monographs on the topic {{< cite "keown1992" "harvey2000" >}}. The sources were cited for passages relevant to the article’s thesis, not through a systematic reading of the monographs’ entire content.

A human verification of the citations on pages 38–39 and 111–121 of Keown and on pages 37–41 of Harvey remains necessary, as does the standardization of all bibliographic entries according to a single standard. The titles, years, publishers, and general bibliographic consistency of the two monographs are consistent with the available bibliographic sources, but verifying individual pages requires direct consultation of the editions used.
